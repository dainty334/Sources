# CHAPTER II

# REVIEW OF RELATED LITERATURE

This section presents the literature and studies that guide the design of DiviRush. It first explains the theories behind learning basic division facts through a quick-tap game, then reviews studies related to each specific objective of the project. It ends with a review of existing applications similar to DiviRush and a synopsis of what the review means for the project.

## Related Literature / Theoretical Background

This section explains the theories that describe how learners may learn from DiviRush's activities. It begins with one anchor theory, retrieval practice, and then adds three supporting bodies of literature: arithmetic fact fluency and working memory, game-based learning and motivation, and feedback and mobile learning. Each supporting body of literature is linked back to the anchor theory.

### Anchor Theory: Retrieval Practice

This section presents retrieval practice as the anchor theory of the project. It explains the theory in plain words, applies it to the activities of DiviRush, and states where the theory does not fully fit.

Retrieval practice is a learning strategy in which learners recall information from memory instead of reading or hearing it again. The benefit of this act of recalling is often called the testing effect, because short tests or quizzes are a common way to make learners retrieve what they know. Agarwal et al. (2021) conducted a systematic review of retrieval practice studies carried out in real schools and classrooms. They screened nearly 2,000 abstracts and coded 50 classroom experiments with 49 effect sizes and a total of 5,374 students. About 57% of the effects showed medium or large benefits of retrieval practice. The benefits held across education levels, content areas, experimental designs, retention delays, test formats, and the timing of practice and feedback. However, the authors also reported that only about 6% of the experiments came from countries outside Western, educated, industrialized, rich, and democratic settings. This limitation means that evidence from schools similar to a Philippine public elementary school is still thin.

A larger synthesis supports the same principle. Yang et al. (2021) carried out a meta-analysis, a study that combines the results of many earlier studies, of 222 independent studies involving 48,478 students. They found that classroom quizzing improved academic achievement to a medium degree (g = 0.499). The size of the effect depended on several conditions, including whether corrective feedback was given, how many times learners were tested, how closely the practice format matched the final test, and how long the treatment lasted. The opened abstract did not report the school levels or subjects covered, so the result applies to classroom learning in general rather than to arithmetic facts in particular.

For arithmetic, McNeil et al. (2025) reviewed what the science of learning says about arithmetic fluency, which they describe as fast and accurate recall of facts together with the flexible use of number relationships. They argued that memorization and thinking strategies are complementary rather than opposing. They recommended well-structured retrieval practice of arithmetic facts and time-limited practice, but only after learners can already answer accurately. This recommendation matters for a timed game, because it places speed after accuracy, not before it.

Retrieval practice applies directly to DiviRush. Every item in the game, such as 56 ÷ 8, asks the learner to recall the quotient from memory before the countdown ends. Each level has 10 items, and levels can be replayed, which gives learners repeated retrieval of the same facts, one of the conditions that Yang et al. (2021) linked to larger effects. When an answer is wrong or time runs out, the game shows the correct answer, which acts as corrective feedback after retrieval. The Easy difficulty gives 15 seconds per item, and a level is passed only with at least 8 correct answers out of 10. These rules follow the advice of McNeil et al. (2025) that learners should be accurate before they are pushed to answer faster.

The anchor theory does not explain every part of DiviRush. First, retrieval practice explains how facts are remembered, but it does not explain why learners would want to keep playing; the points, stars, and unlockable levels need the motivation literature discussed below. Second, time pressure is not part of the classic theory, and McNeil et al. (2025) support timed practice only after accuracy is established. For this reason, DiviRush is a practice resource for facts that learners have already met in class, not a way to teach the meaning of division. Third, DiviRush shows three or four answer bubbles, so each item is partly a recognition task rather than pure free recall. Finally, most retrieval practice research comes from countries unlike the Philippines (Agarwal et al., 2021).

Behaviorism was considered as an alternative anchor. A behaviorist view explains learning through reinforcement, where rewarded responses become more likely. Plass et al. (2015) described a "behaviorist game" that presents a challenge with a limited set of answer choices and gives right or wrong feedback, which closely matches the surface of DiviRush. However, recent evidence treats reinforcement alone as a weak basis for learning. Wisniewski et al. (2020) found that reinforcement or punishment had the smallest effect among feedback types (d = 0.24), and Ryan and Deci (2020) argued that rewards used to control behavior can weaken motivation. For these reasons, this study uses retrieval practice as the anchor and treats behaviorism as a contrasting view.

### Arithmetic Fact Fluency and Working Memory

This section explains why quick recall of division facts matters, using research on working memory and cognitive load. It connects to the anchor theory by showing what learners gain when retrieval of facts becomes fast and accurate.

Working memory is the limited mental space that holds and works with information for a short time. Menon (2016) reviewed how working memory supports children's mathematics learning. The review explained that children who solve simple arithmetic by counting draw on several parts of working memory at once. As children become more skilled and switch from counting to retrieving facts directly from memory, the demand on working memory falls. The review is a narrative summary that relies largely on correlational and brain-imaging studies, and it does not test any practice program, so it explains a mechanism rather than proving that practice works.

Zhang et al. (2023) measured the same relationship in a meta-analysis of 46 studies with 11,224 typically developing children aged 6 to 12. They found a medium correlation between working memory and arithmetic (r = 0.312). Verbal working memory was more strongly related to arithmetic (r = 0.332) than visuospatial working memory (r = 0.297), and all four operations, including division, were related to working memory. The authors noted that their search used only two databases, that the studies were correlational, and that each operation rested on only 7 to 13 effect sizes. Even with these limits, the study covers the same age range as Grade 4 learners.

Cognitive load theory adds an instructional view of the same idea. Sweller et al. (2019) reviewed twenty years of research on this theory, which is based on the observation that new information must pass through a limited working memory before it is stored in long-term memory. Once information is stored in long-term memory, the limits of working memory no longer restrict its use. The opened abstract did not describe specific instructional effects, so this study uses only the general principle.

Together, these sources explain why the anchor theory matters for DiviRush. If repeated retrieval helps a learner store 56 ÷ 8 = 7 in long-term memory, the learner no longer needs to count to find the answer, and working memory is freed for the longer division tasks that Grade 4 learners meet later. The same principle informs the design of the game screen: one item at a time, few answer bubbles, and no unnecessary movement while the timer runs, so that the screen itself does not add to the learner's mental load.

### Game-Based Learning and Motivation

This section explains how game design and motivation theory relate to DiviRush's game elements. It connects to the anchor theory by explaining why learners may return to repeated retrieval practice.

Plass et al. (2015) brought together the cognitive, motivational, affective, and sociocultural foundations of game-based learning. They described a game as a loop of challenge, response, and feedback. One of the learning goals they listed for games is to practice existing knowledge or basic skills "in order to automate them" (p. 264), which is exactly the purpose of DiviRush. They also described the incentive system of a game, such as points, stars, badges, and trophies, as a way to motivate players to continue and to give feedback that shapes behavior (p. 264). At the same time, they noted that such rewards can be extrinsic to the learning content, and that few efforts had applied motivation theory systematically to learning games (p. 268).

Self-determination theory gives a clearer account of how these rewards should work. Ryan and Deci (2020) reviewed twenty years of research on this theory, which states that quality motivation depends on three basic needs: autonomy, or a sense of choice; competence, or a sense of growing skill; and relatedness, or a sense of connection with others. Competence is best supported by well-structured settings with optimal challenge and positive feedback. Feedback that gives useful information supports motivation, while feedback that feels like pressure or control can undermine it. The authors also noted that the theory still needs closer links to specific kinds of learning tasks and that cultures may meet these needs in different ways.

Applied to DiviRush, these sources suggest that points, stars, and unlockable levels should work as information about progress rather than as prizes to control behavior. A learner who earns one star knows that 8 of 10 answers were correct, and a learner who earns three stars knows that every answer was correct. Choosing an episode and a difficulty gives the learner some choice, and the three difficulties offer graded challenge. These features support the anchor theory by encouraging learners to return for more retrieval practice. The literature does not show, however, that these elements will motivate Grade 4 learners in Baluan Elementary School; that remains to be examined.

### Feedback and Mobile Learning

This section reviews the literature on feedback and on learning with mobile devices. Feedback connects to the anchor theory because corrective feedback strengthens retrieval practice, while mobile learning explains the platform through which DiviRush delivers that practice.

Wisniewski et al. (2020) re-examined the effect of feedback in a meta-analysis of 435 studies with about 61,000 participants. The average effect of feedback was d = 0.48, but the results varied widely, so feedback is not one uniform treatment. The type of feedback mattered: reinforcement or punishment had an effect of d = 0.24, corrective feedback had d = 0.46, and high-information feedback had d = 0.99. Feedback also had larger effects on cognitive outcomes than on motivation. The authors warned that some primary data were missing and that learner proficiency and task type were not modeled. For DiviRush, this evidence supports pairing every correct or incorrect mark with the correct answer, rather than relying on points alone.

Sung et al. (2016) studied the effect of integrating mobile devices into teaching and learning in a meta-analysis of 110 experimental and quasi-experimental journal articles published from 1993 to 2013. They found a moderate mean effect of 0.523 on learning performance and noted the potential of mobile devices for learning both inside and outside the classroom. The included studies end in 2013 and used devices such as laptops and personal digital assistants, so the findings may not reflect present-day Android phones and tablets. Still, the study supports examining a mobile application as a channel for practice outside class.

The theories in this section work together. Retrieval practice, the anchor theory, explains how repeated recall strengthens memory for division facts. The working memory literature explains why fast and accurate recall matters for later mathematics. The game-based learning and motivation literature explains why a learner might keep practicing, and the feedback and mobile learning literature explains how each answer is checked and where the practice can happen. The next section reviews empirical studies that relate to each specific objective of DiviRush.

## Related Studies

This section reviews empirical studies related to the four specific objectives of DiviRush, in the same order as the objectives in Chapter I. For each objective, it states what the objective needs, discusses related studies, and explains what the studies mean for the project. It ends with a review of related projects and a synopsis.

### Studies Related to Timed Practice of Division Facts (Objective 1)

This section reviews studies related to the first objective, which is to build a quick-tap game that presents basic division facts in four episodes, each with three difficulties of five levels, with one correct answer among three or four choices answered before a countdown timer ends. The objective needs evidence on digital or game-based practice of basic facts in the elementary grades.

Two meta-analyses set realistic expectations. Tokac et al. (2019) compared mathematics video games with traditional instruction across 24 studies in PreK to Grade 12. They found a small, marginally significant overall effect favoring games (0.13), and neither grade level nor intervention length significantly explained the differences between studies. The authors noted that few studies were rigorous and that most reported their games too poorly to compare game types, including drill-and-practice games. Byun and Joung (2018) also reviewed digital game-based learning in K–12 mathematics; of 296 studies found, 33 were analyzed for trends and 17 for effect size. Its overall effect size was not shown on the opened record, so it is used here only as further evidence that digital mathematics games are an active area of research.

The closest study to DiviRush is that of Jay et al. (2019), who designed and tested a tablet game for fast recall of arithmetic facts. In a quasi-experimental crossover design, 97 children aged 7 to 8 in two primary schools in England played the game for 20 minutes a day for two weeks. The game group gained 10.65 points on a one-minute basic facts test, compared with 6.20 points for the control group, an effect the authors reported as d = 0.6. Play on the game's multiples activities was also related to gains in division (r = .268). However, the authors noted that several design features were changed at once, that the groups may have differed at the start, and that the test did not separate speed from accuracy. The learners were also younger than Grade 4 learners.

Berrett and Carter (2018) examined a commercial computer program for multiplication facts with 63 third-grade students. Using a multiple-baseline design across three groups over 12 weeks, they found that all groups improved in multiplication fact fluency after the program began and that the gains were maintained at follow-up. The study had a small sample, no comparison program, and no reported effect sizes, and the opened record did not name the country.

Two Philippine studies involve Grade 4 learners. Camarillo (2026) compared game-based and drill-based multiplication instruction with 360 Grade 4 learners in 12 public elementary schools in Bauang North District, La Union. After four weeks, the game group improved significantly more than the drill group (d = 0.85), and the share of learners at the Proficient or Advanced level rose from 15.56% to 48.33%, compared with 16.67% to 28.89% in the drill group. The games appear to have been classroom card and dice games, the study did not use random assignment, and it lasted only four weeks. Cayang and Ursabia (2024) tested game-based learning with Grade 4 pupils at an elementary school in Aleosan, North Cotabato, a setting in the same region as Baluan Elementary School. The experimental group scored higher on the post-test (M = 19.18) than the control group (M = 16.31), a significant difference (p = .022). The opened record did not report the sample size or describe the games, and the study took place in one school.

These studies inform the design of DiviRush in three ways. First, game-based practice of basic facts has produced gains in several settings, but the pooled effect of mathematics games is small (Tokac et al., 2019), so DiviRush should be treated as a supplement to classroom teaching. Second, the closest digital study involved multiplication and division recall but with younger English learners (Jay et al., 2019), while the Philippine Grade 4 studies involved non-digital or unspecified games and topics other than division. Third, none of the reviewed studies tested a timed, multiple-choice game for division facts with Filipino Grade 4 learners, which is a limit of the reviewed literature rather than proof that such a game does not exist.

### Studies Related to Mobile Practice Outside the Classroom (Objective 2)

This section reviews studies related to the second objective, which is to provide short, replayable levels of 10 items each that a learner can complete in one sitting on an Android phone or tablet outside the classroom. The objective needs evidence on mathematics practice with mobile devices, especially at home and in short sessions.

Berkowitz et al. (2015) tested whether a parent–child mathematics activity delivered through a tablet application at home could raise achievement. In a randomized field experiment with 587 first-grade children and their parents in the United States, children who used the mathematics app across one school year improved more in mathematics than children who used a reading app. The gains were larger for children whose parents were anxious about mathematics. Because the app worked through parent–child interaction, its effect cannot be separated from parent involvement, and the children were much younger than Grade 4 learners.

Pitchford (2015) tested curriculum-aligned mathematics apps on tablets in a low-income setting. In a randomized controlled trial at one urban primary school in Lilongwe, Malawi, 283 children in Standards 1 to 3 were analyzed across three groups: a mathematics tablet group, a non-mathematics tablet group, and normal classroom practice. After eight weeks, the mathematics tablet group outperformed both control groups in Standards 2 and 3, with effect sizes as large as d = 1.698 in Standard 3, while Standard 1 showed no significant difference. The author noted limited statistical power, the use of tablet-based tests, and a single school.

Outhwaite et al. (2019) ran a pupil-level randomized controlled trial of interactive mathematics apps with 389 children aged 4 to 5 in the United Kingdom. Over 12 weeks, both app conditions, whether added to standard activities or used in place of a small-group activity, produced larger mathematics gains than standard practice alone, and the two app conditions did not differ. The children were very young, and the apps were used only in school.

In the Philippines, Pilon and Ruales (2024) studied teacher-made digital games with 35 Grade 3 learners at Maitum Elementary School in SY 2022–2023. Using a single-group pretest–posttest design, they reported a significant difference between pretest and posttest scores and noted greater engagement and enjoyment. The study had no control group, a small sample, and no reported test statistics, and the opened record did not state the device used.

These studies support examining short, app-based mathematics practice as a supplement to teaching. The strongest designs were randomized trials (Berkowitz et al., 2015; Outhwaite et al., 2019; Pitchford, 2015), but they involved younger children and general early mathematics rather than Grade 4 division facts. Only one study involved home use, and it depended on parents (Berkowitz et al., 2015). The reviewed studies therefore do not show whether Grade 4 learners will practice on their own at home, which DiviRush's offline Android design is meant to allow and which remains to be tested.

### Studies Related to Game Elements (Objective 3)

This section reviews studies related to the third objective, which is to include game elements, namely a running score with speed and streak bonuses, one to three stars per level, a saved best score and time, bubble-style visuals with sound, and level unlocking based on stars. The objective needs evidence on how such elements affect learners, including mixed or negative findings.

Jagušt et al. (2018) compared competitive, collaborative, and adaptive gamification with a non-gamified version of mathematics activities on tablets in two Grade 2 classes and one Grade 3 class. Overall, the gamified activities improved mathematics performance, and the best results came from a condition that combined competition, narrative, and adaptivity. However, the highest performance came with the most incorrect attempts, while the number of correct attempts did not change. The authors concluded that success depends on how game elements are balanced, not on any single element. The opened record did not report the total number of participants, and performance was measured only from game logs.

Karamert and Kuyumcu Vardar (2021) tested gamification with 46 fifth-grade students in a fractions unit in Turkey. After six weeks of progress maps and weekly achievements, the gamified class scored significantly higher on the achievement test, but attitudes toward mathematics did not differ between the groups. The game elements were deliberately non-digital, and the study covered one region and one school year.

Two meta-analyses show how results vary. Kurnaz and Koçtürk (2025) combined 31 studies with 41 effect sizes and found an overall effect of gamification on K–12 motivation of g = 0.654, but the effect was smallest for primary students (g = 0.309), and heterogeneity was high. Diaz and Estoque-Loñez (2024), authors from De La Salle University, combined 15 quasi-experimental studies from Asia published from 2018 to 2022, 60% of them from Southeast Asia. They found a large overall effect on achievement (g = 1.30), with no significant differences by school level, discipline, or platform. However, only one of the included studies was about mathematics, and about 40% involved tertiary students.

Because DiviRush uses a countdown timer, studies on timed tasks are also relevant. Hallez and Vallier (2025) tested a visible timer during timed mathematics tests with 44 children aged 7 to 9 in France. Performance did not differ with or without the visible timer (d = −0.067), but anticipatory anxiety was lower (d = 0.42) and inattentive behavior was less frequent with the visible timer. The sample was small, the children were tested individually, and the task was on paper rather than on a screen.

These studies inform the design of DiviRush's game elements. Game elements can improve performance, but the effects are smaller at the primary level (Kurnaz & Koçtürk, 2025), they may not change attitudes (Karamert & Kuyumcu Vardar, 2021), and speed-oriented rewards may raise attempts without raising accuracy (Jagušt et al., 2018). For this reason, DiviRush awards stars for correct answers only and gives speed and streak bonuses as points, so that accuracy, not speed alone, decides whether a level is passed. The finding of Hallez and Vallier (2025) supports DiviRush's visible timer bar, while also showing that a timer by itself does not raise performance.

### Studies Related to Immediate Feedback (Objective 4)

This section reviews studies related to the fourth objective, which is to display immediate visual feedback that marks each answer as correct or incorrect, and shows the correct answer when time runs out, before the next item appears. The objective needs evidence on the kind and timing of feedback in digital mathematics practice for children.

Van der Kleij et al. (2015) combined 40 studies with 70 effect sizes on item-based feedback in computer-based learning environments. Elaborated feedback had the largest effect (0.49), followed by knowledge of the correct response (0.32), while knowledge of result only, that is, marking an answer right or wrong, had almost no effect (0.05). Effects were larger in mathematics than in other subjects, delayed feedback lowered the effects, and primary and high school levels were associated with smaller effects. The studies covered mixed subjects and ages.

Fyfe and Rittle-Johnson (2016) tested whether children's prior knowledge decides whether feedback helps. In two randomized experiments with 108 and 101 second- and third-grade children in Nashville, United States, feedback helped children who had not been taught a correct strategy but hurt children who had already been taught one. The task was mathematical equivalence rather than fact recall, and the feedback came from both a person and a computer.

Merrick and Fyfe (2024) studied how feedback content and source affect children's persistence. In a randomized study with 130 children aged 6 to 9 in the United States, children who were shown only the correct answer persisted longer than children who also received a right-or-wrong mark, an effect driven by low-knowledge children (14.7 vs. 8.7 items). Feedback from the computer alone also produced higher accuracy than feedback from a computer and a person together. The sessions were held online at home, the sample was small, and no learning post-test was given.

Cubillos et al. (2023) compared two kinds of feedback in a drill-and-practice mathematics game with 73 primary students in two schools in Chile. Students who could try each item up to three times showed greater learning gains and higher perceived competence and autonomy than students who received the correct response after one try, but they also reported higher perceived pressure. The opened record did not report test statistics or grade levels.

These studies inform the design of DiviRush's feedback. Marking an answer only as right or wrong has little effect (Van der Kleij et al., 2015), so DiviRush also shows the correct answer when an item is missed. Feedback may help weaker learners more than learners who already know the facts (Fyfe & Rittle-Johnson, 2016), and a red mark may lower the persistence of low-knowledge learners (Merrick & Fyfe, 2024). These findings support examining whether DiviRush's wrong-answer signal should stay gentle, and whether a retry option, which Cubillos et al. (2023) linked to greater learning but also to more pressure, should be considered in a later version.

### Related Projects

This section describes three existing applications similar to DiviRush and compares them with the proposed project. All product information comes from the developers' official pages or App Store listings, so it describes features, not evidence that the products improve learning.

Times Tables Rock Stars is a times tables program by Maths Circle Ltd in the United Kingdom (Maths Circle Ltd, n.d.). It runs in a web browser and as a mobile app and is described as suitable for learners aged 6 and up. Its stated aim is to build fluency and recall in multiplication and division through short, quick-fire questions, with multiplayer games, leaderboards, and avatars. The app is free to download but requires a subscription. No effectiveness study of the program was found.

XtraMath is a nonprofit online program for basic facts in addition, subtraction, multiplication, and division, designed for Kindergarten to Grade 6 (XtraMath, n.d.). It offers short daily practice sessions of about 10 minutes that adapt to each learner, with progress reports for teachers. Wells (n.d.), an action research study run in spring 2015 with 36 fourth-grade students in Illinois, found that learners who used XtraMath and learners who used flash cards and board games improved by similar amounts, with no significant overall difference. The study was small, short, and not peer reviewed.

The Mathplus project of the Department of Mathematics at Ateneo de Manila University offers free Android apps aligned with the DepEd curriculum, including a Multiplication Game and a Divisibility Game (Mathplus Resources, n.d.). De Las Peñas et al. (2023) described the design of these apps, which build multiplication and divisibility concepts through skip counting, arrays, and divisibility rules and give immediate feedback. Their paper reported that learners' access to devices was one of the hardest problems in rolling out the apps. It is a design paper written by the developers and reports no learner outcome data.

### Synopsis

This section brings together the theories, studies, and projects reviewed in this chapter. It explains what the review suggests for the design of DiviRush, what the evidence does not show, and what still needs to be tested.

The reviewed theories suggest a consistent design for DiviRush. Retrieval practice supports repeated recall of division facts with corrective feedback (Agarwal et al., 2021; Yang et al., 2021), and the arithmetic fluency literature supports timed practice only after accuracy is established (McNeil et al., 2025). The working memory literature explains why fast recall of facts may free mental resources for later mathematics (Menon, 2016; Zhang et al., 2023). The game-based learning and motivation literature suggests that points, stars, and unlocking should give learners information about their progress (Plass et al., 2015; Ryan & Deci, 2020), and the feedback literature suggests that the correct answer should always be shown after an error (Van der Kleij et al., 2015; Wisniewski et al., 2020).

The reviewed studies also show the limits of this evidence. The pooled effect of mathematics games is small (Tokac et al., 2019), game elements have smaller effects on motivation at the primary level (Kurnaz & Koçtürk, 2025), and some elements raised attempts without raising accuracy (Jagušt et al., 2018). Feedback can hinder learners who already know a strategy (Fyfe & Rittle-Johnson, 2016), and a right-or-wrong mark can reduce the persistence of weaker learners (Merrick & Fyfe, 2024). Many of the studies had small samples, short durations, single schools, or younger learners, and several of the Philippine studies did not report key details.

Within the reviewed studies, there is a specific gap. The closest digital study of fact recall involved younger learners in England (Jay et al., 2019). The Philippine Grade 4 studies used classroom games or unspecified activities and focused on multiplication or general mathematics (Camarillo, 2026; Cayang & Ursabia, 2024). The related projects that cover division facts are online or subscription-based, and the Philippine apps build multiplication and divisibility concepts rather than timed recall of division facts. None of the reviewed studies examined an offline, timed, multiple-choice game for basic division facts with Grade 4 learners in a Philippine public school. This is a limit of the reviewed literature, not a claim that no such game exists.

Several questions remain open for DiviRush. The accuracy of the division items and their fit with the MATATAG curriculum must be checked by teachers. The game must be tested on the Android phones and tablets that learners actually use. The timer lengths of 15, 10, and 7 seconds are starting values that need to be tried with Grade 4 learners. Finally, whether DiviRush improves division fact recall can only be answered by testing it with learners, because the studies reviewed here inform its design but do not prove that it will work.
