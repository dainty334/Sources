# CHAPTER II

# REVIEW OF RELATED LITERATURE

This section presents the literature and studies that guide the design of DiviRush. It first explains the theories behind learning basic division facts through a quick-tap game, then reviews studies related to each specific objective of the project. It ends with a review of three similar applications and a summary of what the review means for the project.

## Related Literature / Theoretical Background

This section explains the theories that describe how learners may learn from DiviRush's activities. It begins with one anchor theory, retrieval practice, and then adds three supporting bodies of literature: arithmetic fact fluency and working memory, game-based learning and motivation, and feedback and mobile learning.

The anchor theory of this project is retrieval practice, a learning strategy in which learners recall information from memory instead of reading or hearing it again. Its benefit is often called the testing effect, because short quizzes are a common way to make learners retrieve what they know. The idea is simple: each time a learner pulls a fact out of memory, that fact becomes easier to recall the next time.

Agarwal et al. (2021) reviewed 50 classroom experiments with 5,374 students and found medium or large benefits of retrieval practice in about 57% of the effects, across education levels, subjects, and test formats. However, only about 6% of the experiments came from non-Western settings, so evidence from schools like Baluan Elementary School is still thin. Yang et al. (2021) combined 222 studies with 48,478 students and found that classroom quizzing improved achievement to a medium degree (g = 0.499), with larger effects when corrective feedback was given and when learners were tested repeatedly. For arithmetic, McNeil et al. (2025) recommended structured retrieval practice of facts, with time-limited practice only after learners can already answer accurately.

Retrieval practice applies directly to DiviRush. Every item, such as 56 ÷ 8, asks the learner to recall the quotient from memory before the countdown ends. Each level has 10 items and can be replayed, so the same facts are retrieved many times. When an answer is wrong or time runs out, the game shows the correct answer, which acts as corrective feedback. The Easy difficulty allows 15 seconds per item, and a level is passed only with at least 8 correct answers out of 10, so accuracy comes before speed, as McNeil et al. (2025) advised.

The anchor theory does not explain every part of DiviRush. It explains how facts are remembered, but not why learners would want to keep playing; the points, stars, and unlockable levels need the motivation literature below. Time pressure is not part of the classic theory, and DiviRush shows three or four answer bubbles, so each item is partly a recognition task rather than pure recall. For these reasons, DiviRush is a practice resource for facts that learners have already met in class, not a way to teach the meaning of division.

Behaviorism was considered as an alternative anchor, because it explains learning through rewarded responses. Plass et al. (2015) described a "behaviorist game" with a limited set of answer choices and right-or-wrong feedback, which closely matches the surface of DiviRush. However, Wisniewski et al. (2020) found that reinforcement or punishment was the weakest type of feedback (d = 0.24), and Ryan and Deci (2020) warned that rewards used to control behavior can weaken motivation. For this reason, retrieval practice is used as the anchor, and behaviorism is treated as a contrasting view.

The first supporting body of literature, on arithmetic fact fluency and working memory, explains why quick recall of division facts matters. It connects to the anchor theory by showing what learners gain when retrieval becomes fast and accurate. Working memory is the limited mental space that holds and works with information for a short time. When a learner solves 56 ÷ 8 by counting or repeated subtraction, that space fills up quickly. When the learner simply remembers that 56 ÷ 8 = 7, much of that space stays free.

Menon (2016) explained that children who count to solve simple arithmetic draw on several parts of working memory, and that this demand falls when they switch to retrieving facts; the review is narrative and relies mostly on correlational and brain-imaging data. Zhang et al. (2023) combined 46 studies with 11,224 children aged 6 to 12 and found a medium link between working memory and arithmetic (r = 0.312) for all four operations, including division. Their studies were correlational and came from only two databases. Sweller et al. (2019) summarized cognitive load theory, which holds that new information passes through a limited working memory before it is stored in long-term memory, where those limits no longer apply.

Together, these sources explain why the anchor theory matters. If repeated retrieval helps a learner store division facts in long-term memory, the learner no longer needs to count, and working memory is freed for the longer division that Grade 4 learners meet later in the school year. The same principle informs the game screen of DiviRush, which shows one item and only a few answer bubbles at a time, so that the screen itself does not add to the learner's mental load.

The second supporting body of literature, on game-based learning and motivation, relates to DiviRush's game elements and explains why learners may return to repeated retrieval practice.

Plass et al. (2015) described learning games as a loop of challenge, response, and feedback, and listed practicing basic skills "in order to automate them" as one goal of games (p. 264). They also noted that points, stars, and badges motivate players but can be extrinsic to the learning content (p. 264). Ryan and Deci (2020) explained that quality motivation depends on autonomy, competence, and relatedness, and that informative feedback supports motivation while controlling rewards can undermine it. They also noted that different cultures may meet these needs in different ways.

Applied to DiviRush, these sources suggest that game elements should tell learners how they are doing rather than simply reward them. A learner who earns one star knows that 8 of 10 answers were correct, and a learner who earns three stars knows that every answer was correct. Choosing an episode and a difficulty gives the learner some choice, and the three difficulties offer graded challenge. These features may encourage learners to return for more retrieval practice, but the literature does not show that they will motivate Grade 4 learners at Baluan Elementary School; that remains to be examined.

The third supporting body of literature covers feedback, which strengthens the anchor theory, and mobile learning, which explains the platform through which DiviRush delivers practice.

Wisniewski et al. (2020) combined 435 studies and found an average feedback effect of d = 0.48, with corrective feedback (d = 0.46) much stronger than reinforcement alone (d = 0.24). The results varied widely, so feedback is not one uniform treatment. Sung et al. (2016) combined 110 studies of mobile devices in teaching and found a moderate effect on learning performance (0.523). Their studies ended in 2013, before present-day Android phones and tablets were common in schools.

For DiviRush, the feedback evidence supports pairing every correct or incorrect mark with the correct answer instead of relying on points alone. The mobile learning evidence supports examining an Android application as a way to practice both inside and outside the classroom.

The theories in this chapter work together. Retrieval practice explains how repeated recall strengthens memory for division facts, and the working memory literature explains why fast recall matters for later mathematics. The game-based learning and motivation literature explains why a learner might keep practicing, and the feedback and mobile learning literature explains how each answer is checked and where practice can happen. The next section reviews empirical studies related to each specific objective of DiviRush.

## Related Studies

This section reviews empirical studies related to the four specific objectives of DiviRush, in the same order as the objectives in Chapter I. For each objective, it states what the objective needs, summarizes related studies, and explains what they mean for the project. It ends with a review of three related projects and a summary of the chapter.

The first objective of DiviRush is a quick-tap game that presents basic division facts in four episodes, each with three difficulties of five levels, with one correct answer among three or four choices answered before a countdown ends. The objective needs evidence on digital or game-based practice of basic facts in the elementary grades.

Tokac et al. (2019) combined 24 studies from preschool to Grade 12 and found only a small effect of mathematics games over traditional instruction (0.13). Most of those studies described their games too poorly to compare game types. Byun and Joung (2018) also reviewed digital game-based learning in K–12 mathematics, using 17 studies for effect size, although the overall effect was not shown on the opened record.

Jay et al. (2019) found that 97 children aged 7 to 8 in England who played a tablet fact-recall game for two weeks gained more on a basic facts test than a control group (d = 0.6). The study changed several design features at once and involved learners younger than Grade 4. Berrett and Carter (2018) found that 63 Grade 3 students improved in multiplication fact fluency with a computer program over 12 weeks and kept their gains at follow-up. The study was small and had no comparison program.

In the Philippines, Camarillo (2026) found that 360 Grade 4 learners in 12 schools in La Union gained more fact mastery from multiplication games than from timed drill (d = 0.85). The games appear to have been non-digital, and the study lasted only four weeks. Cayang and Ursabia (2024) found that Grade 4 pupils in Aleosan, North Cotabato, who learned through games scored higher on a post-test than a control group (p = .022). The opened record did not report the sample size or describe the games.

These studies support examining game-based fact practice as a supplement to classroom teaching. The small pooled effect reported by Tokac et al. (2019) keeps expectations modest. None of the reviewed studies tested a timed, multiple-choice division game with Filipino Grade 4 learners, which is a limit of the reviewed literature rather than proof that no such game exists.

The second objective of DiviRush is to provide short, replayable levels of 10 items each that a learner can complete in one sitting on an Android phone or tablet outside the classroom. The objective needs evidence on mathematics practice with mobile devices, especially at home and in short sessions.

Berkowitz et al. (2015) found in a randomized experiment with 587 first graders in the United States that a parent–child mathematics app used at home improved achievement over one school year. Its effect cannot be separated from parent involvement. Pitchford (2015) found that 283 children in the first three grades of a school in Malawi who used curriculum-aligned tablet apps for eight weeks outperformed control groups in Standards 2 and 3. The trial took place in one school and used tablet-based tests.

Outhwaite et al. (2019) found in a randomized trial with 389 children aged 4 to 5 in the United Kingdom that mathematics apps produced larger gains than standard practice. The apps were used only in school with very young children. In the Philippines, Pilon and Ruales (2024) reported higher post-test scores and more engagement among 35 Grade 3 learners at Maitum Elementary School who used teacher-made digital games. The study had no control group and reported no statistics.

These studies support examining short, app-based mathematics practice as a supplement to teaching. However, all of them involved learners younger than Grade 4, and only one involved home use, which depended on parents. Whether Grade 4 learners will practice on their own at home with an offline Android game remains to be tested.

The third objective of DiviRush is to include game elements, namely a running score with speed and streak bonuses, one to three stars per level, a saved best score and time, bubble-style visuals with sound, and level unlocking based on stars. The objective needs evidence on how such elements affect learners, including mixed findings.

Jagušt et al. (2018) found that gamified tablet activities improved the mathematics performance of Grade 2 and 3 learners, but the best performance came with more incorrect attempts, not more correct ones. Performance was measured only from game logs. Karamert and Kuyumcu Vardar (2021) found that gamification improved the achievement of 46 Grade 5 students in Turkey but did not change their attitudes toward mathematics. The game elements in that study were non-digital.

Kurnaz and Koçtürk (2025) combined 31 studies and found that gamification raised K–12 motivation (g = 0.654), but the effect was smallest in primary school (g = 0.309). Diaz and Estoque-Loñez (2024), from De La Salle University, combined 15 Asian studies and found a large effect of gamification on achievement (g = 1.30), although only one of those studies was about mathematics. Hallez and Vallier (2025) found that a visible timer lowered the anxiety of 44 children aged 7 to 9 in France without changing their mathematics scores. Their sample was small, and the task was on paper.

These studies suggest that game elements can help, but not uniformly, and that speed-based rewards may raise attempts without raising accuracy. For this reason, DiviRush awards stars for correct answers only and gives speed and streak bonuses as points, so accuracy decides whether a level is passed. The timer study supports DiviRush's visible timer bar, while showing that a timer alone does not raise performance.

The fourth objective of DiviRush is to display immediate visual feedback that marks each answer as correct or incorrect, and shows the correct answer when time runs out, before the next item appears. The objective needs evidence on the kind and timing of feedback in digital mathematics practice.

Van der Kleij et al. (2015) combined 40 studies and found that marking answers only as right or wrong had almost no effect (0.05), while showing the correct response (0.32) or elaborated feedback (0.49) helped more. The studies covered mixed subjects and ages. Fyfe and Rittle-Johnson (2016) found in two experiments with Grade 2 and 3 children in the United States that feedback helped children without a strategy but hurt those who already had one. Their task was mathematical equivalence rather than fact recall.

Merrick and Fyfe (2024) found that 130 children aged 6 to 9 in the United States persisted longer when shown only the correct answer than when also given a right-or-wrong mark, especially low-knowledge children. The sessions were held online and included no learning post-test. Cubillos et al. (2023) found that 73 primary students in Chile learned more when allowed several tries per item, but they also felt more pressure. The opened record gave no test statistics.

These studies support showing the correct answer after every missed item, as DiviRush does, rather than marking answers only right or wrong. They also suggest examining whether DiviRush's wrong-answer signal should stay gentle for weaker learners, and whether a retry option should be considered in a later version.

Three existing applications similar to DiviRush were also reviewed and compared with the proposed project. All product information comes from the developers' official pages, so it describes features, not evidence that the products improve learning.

Times Tables Rock Stars, by Maths Circle Ltd (n.d.), is a subscription program for learners aged 6 and up that builds multiplication and division recall through quick-fire questions, multiplayer games, and leaderboards. XtraMath (n.d.) is a free online program for Kindergarten to Grade 6 that gives about 10 minutes of daily adaptive practice in basic facts, including division. Wells (n.d.) found in an action research study with 36 Grade 4 students in Illinois that learners using XtraMath and learners using flash cards improved by similar amounts; the study was small and not peer reviewed.

The Mathplus apps of Ateneo de Manila University (Mathplus Resources, n.d.) are free Android games for multiplication and divisibility concepts aligned with the DepEd curriculum. De Las Peñas et al. (2023) described their design and reported that learners' access to devices was a major problem, but they reported no learner outcomes.

The comparison shows that DiviRush shares timed fact practice and immediate feedback with these projects. It differs in its narrow focus on division facts with divisors and quotients from 1 to 10, its fully offline Android design, and its users, the Grade 4 learners of Baluan Elementary School. These differences describe the proposed design only, not evidence that DiviRush works better.

Taken together, the theories, studies, and projects reviewed in this chapter suggest a consistent design for DiviRush. Retrieval practice supports repeated recall of division facts with corrective feedback (Agarwal et al., 2021; Yang et al., 2021), timed practice should follow accuracy (McNeil et al., 2025), and fast recall may free working memory for later mathematics (Zhang et al., 2023). Points and stars should inform learners about their progress (Ryan & Deci, 2020), and the correct answer should be shown after every error (Van der Kleij et al., 2015).

The reviewed evidence also has limits. The pooled effect of mathematics games is small (Tokac et al., 2019), gamification has smaller effects at the primary level (Kurnaz & Koçtürk, 2025), and feedback can hinder learners who already know a strategy (Fyfe & Rittle-Johnson, 2016). Many studies had small samples, short durations, single schools, or younger learners, and several Philippine studies did not report key details.

Within the reviewed studies, none examined an offline, timed, multiple-choice game for basic division facts with Grade 4 learners in a Philippine public school. The closest digital study involved younger learners in England (Jay et al., 2019), and the Philippine Grade 4 studies used classroom games or other topics (Camarillo, 2026; Cayang & Ursabia, 2024). This is a limit of the reviewed literature, not a claim that no such game exists.

Several questions remain open. Teachers must check the accuracy of the division items and their fit with the MATATAG curriculum, and the game must be tested on the Android devices that learners actually use. The timer lengths of 15, 10, and 7 seconds need to be tried with Grade 4 learners. Finally, whether DiviRush improves division fact recall can only be answered by testing it with learners, because the studies reviewed here inform its design but do not prove that it will work.
